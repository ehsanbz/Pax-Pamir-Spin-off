Historical narrative

1747 to the 1820s

After empire: several centres of power

The story does not begin with the borders of today's states. The Durrani polity founded by Ahmad Shah in Kandahar came under pressure from succession disputes and rival chiefs in the early nineteenth century. Dost Mohammad gained power in Kabul, the Barakzai brothers held influence in Kandahar, and Herat was governed separately. Shah Shuja, a displaced claimant, still possessed a name and a network that could be put to political use. This world should be understood as a set of centres of power negotiating and fighting over revenue, routes, and sovereignty.

  2. Encyclopaedia Iranica: Abdali / Durrani
1809 to 1837

Punjab: a power with independent aims

Lahore was more than a peripheral stage for Afghan politics. Ranjit Singh built a powerful state that employed officers and administrators of diverse backgrounds. Sikh expansion into Peshawar made Dost Mohammad's wish to recover it a diplomatic issue. Meanwhile, Allard's and Avitabile's service and Zorawar's campaigns show that military knowledge and regional ambition travelled along paths beyond Anglo-Russian rivalry. The Koh-i-Noor also recalls how jewels, sheltering a king, and claims to legitimacy could become intertwined.

  1. Croatian Encyclopaedia: Ranjit Singh
  2. Royal Collection Trust: Koh-i-Noor
1828 to 1838

From Tabriz and Tehran to Herat's walls

In Iran, defeat by Russia and the Qajar succession question made foreign policy inseparable from court politics. Abbas Mirza died before taking the throne; his son Mohammad Shah succeeded Fath-Ali Shah, and Qa'em-Maqam, who had helped establish him, was killed on his orders. Aghasi then rose to power. The siege of Herat brought together Qajar ambitions to extend authority, the local government's resistance, and foreign agents' involvement. Simonich and Pottinger mattered, but neither the city nor the Iranian state was a passive stage at their disposal.

  1. Encyclopaedia Iranica: Mohammad Shah
  2. Encyclopaedia Iranica: the Herat question
1837 to 1842

Kabul: negotiation, occupation, and uprising

Burnes's and Witkiewicz's talks with Dost Mohammad made Kabul a hub of intelligence and diplomacy. The outcome was no stable alliance for the emir: Britain's decision to restore Shah Shuja was implemented through the invasion of 1839. Initial military success did not resolve questions of legitimacy, livelihood, and government. The 1841 uprising, the deaths of Burnes and Macnaghten, and the disastrous winter retreat of 1842 exposed the limits of occupation. Afghans did not share a single position either; Jan-Fishan, Aminullah, and Akbar Khan followed different political paths. Dost Mohammad's return after the foreign troops' withdrawal opened fresh domestic rivalries.

  1. Encyclopaedia Iranica: Anglo-Afghan Wars
  2. Contemporary letters and memoirs; Library of Congress description
1839 to 1845

Bukhara, Khiva, and Kokand: a parallel story

In the north, khans and emirs pursued their own aims. Perovsky's campaign from Orenburg to Khiva failed against cold and logistical collapse before reaching its destination. Nasrullah fought Kokand, and Madali was killed in 1842; Stoddart and Conolly were also executed in Bukhara that year. Wolff's subsequent journey gave the two officers' story prominence in European memory, but Central Asian history is not confined to foreign agents' lives. Murad Beg's Kunduz, trade networks, and the lives of communities and enslaved people also belonged to this world.

  1. Alexander Morrison: the winter invasion of Khiva
  2. Jeff Eden, Slavery and Empire in Central Asia, Harvard dissertation
Beyond the core narrative

Everyday life and the cards' different historical times

Qanats, wheat, bazaars, and nomadism supplied the material foundations of politics. Most of these subjects have no single-day beginning or end. The Minaret of Jam comes from the twelfth century; Hayward travelled in the late 1860s, and the famous Panjdeh crisis belongs to 1885. Flashman is a literary character altogether. This research therefore does not treat the cards as an entirely simultaneous calendar. Generic event titles are linked to a particular occurrence only where that connection is documented; a thematic connection is different from historical identity.

1. Mohan Lal Kashmiri

Mohan Lal · Card region: Kabul

1812–1877

1831–1846; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Mohan Lal was a Delhi-educated man of Kashmiri descent whose command of languages and knowledge of local networks made him an important intermediary in the East India Company's travels and diplomacy. His work alongside Alexander Burnes on journeys through Afghanistan and Central Asia involved more than translation: it included gathering intelligence, learning about trade routes, and recording observations. His writings show how the production of colonial knowledge depended on Indian scholars and intermediaries.

For the 1839 campaign, the National Army Museum highlights his role in obtaining intelligence about one of the gates of Ghazni. He later wrote a biography of Dost Mohammad. His ties to Burnes and Macnaghten were professional relationships within the British establishment, rather than evidence that their views entirely coincided. His writings are important evidence, but his claims about his own role and his opponents need to be compared with Afghan sources.

Related places: Delhi, Kabul, Bukhara, Ghazni

2. Jan-Fishan Khan

Jan-Fishan Khan · Card region: Kabul

Birth date unknown; died 1864

1839–1857; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

His birth year is unknown; biographical sources give 1864 as the year of his death.

Sayyid Mohammad Khan of Paghman appears in memoirs of the First Afghan War under the title Jan-Fishan Khan. He was among the local chiefs who sided with Shah Shujah and British forces. In her account of the siege, Florentia Sale describes his cooperation with Robert Sale's troops in Kohistan and his loyalty to the shah and the British. The admiring language of this contemporary testimony reflects the author's position within the British camp.

His story is a reminder that Afghan society did not respond to foreign intervention with a single position: family ties, local interests, and competition for court patronage produced different choices. In the relationship network, he is linked to Shah Shujah through political and military support. His later activities in India extend beyond the central focus of the 1839–1842 war, and his position should not be taken as representative of all Afghans.

Related places: Paghman, Kabul, Jalalabad, Sardhana

3. Prince Akbar Khan (Muhammad Akbar Khan)

Prince Akbar Khan · Card region: Kabul

Accounts of his lifespan differ; approximately the 1810s to the mid-1840s

1837–1843; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

NAM gives a lifespan of 1813–1845, while Iranica's entry on Afghan historiography gives 1816–1847; exact life dates should not be presented as settled.

Muhammad Akbar Khan, Dost Mohammad's son, became a central figure in resistance to the occupation. While his father was detained in India, power was divided among Shah Shujah, foreign forces, and insurgent chiefs. Akbar Khan entered this contest with military force and the name of the Barakzai family behind him, but the uprising's many actions should not all be understood as unified obedience to him.

On 23 December 1841, Macnaghten was killed during a meeting with him. Following the agreement to withdraw, the retreat of January 1842 ended in disaster, and a number of captives, including Pottinger, remained in his custody. British sources often present him as responsible for the catastrophe, whereas works such as the Akbarnama emphasize his resistance and dynastic standing. Both responsibility for violence and hostage-taking and the context of occupation and competition for power need to be considered.

Related places: Kabul, Jalalabad, Jamrud

4. Charles Stoddart

Charles Stoddart · Card region: Kabul

1806–1842

1838–1842; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Stoddart was a British officer and envoy whose mission extended from the sphere of Iran and Herat to Bukhara. He went to Nasrullah Khan in December 1838 and was taken prisoner. European accounts attribute the emir's anger to disagreements over reception etiquette and political suspicion; these explanations should be read in the context of foreign powers' efforts to gain influence in Bukhara.

Arthur Conolly's arrival in 1841 did not secure Stoddart's release, and both men were imprisoned. Their letters from January to May 1842 survive in the British Library and provide direct evidence of their final months. Joseph Wolff later travelled to establish their fate. The prisoners' own letters, collected testimony, and the dramatic details of later narratives need to be distinguished.

Related places: Herat, Bukhara

5. Shah Shujah Durrani

Shah Shujah Durrani · Card region: Kabul

Died 5 April 1842; first reign 1803/1804–1809

1803–1842; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Sources date the beginning of his first reign to either 1803 or 1804.

Shah Shujah belonged to the Sadozai branch of the Durrani family and had ruled Kabul before Dost Mohammad's rise. After losing the throne, he spent years in exile; his restoration in 1839 represented British use of an established dynastic claimant. His alliance with Ranjit Singh and the Government of India tied his claim to the interests of powers whose aims did not fully coincide.

Returning with a foreign army weakened his legitimacy and did not eliminate Barakzai and local opposition. Even after the garrison withdrew, he was not safe in the Bala Hissar and was killed in April 1842. Memoirs attributed to him and Afghan histories offer access to the claimant's perspective. It is necessary to look beyond the one-dimensional image of a “British puppet” without overlooking the military dependence of his restoration.

Related places: Kabul, Ludhiana, Lahore, Kandahar

6. Aminullah Khan Logari

Aminullah Khan Logari · Card region: Kabul

His life dates have not been established with confidence

1841–1843; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Reliable, accessible independent biographical material is limited; unsupported dates have not been included.

Aminullah Khan Logari was an influential Afghan notable in Logar and the area around Kabul who played a role in the crisis of occupation and the uprising of 1841. The standing of such leaders depended on landholding, local administration, and long-established dynastic ties. Control over passes, revenue, and local armed forces enabled them to act independently; they were not merely instruments of the great powers.

Christine Noelle's research places him among those who gained standing during the uprising and its aftermath. After Dost Mohammad's return, participation in resistance did not guarantee political loyalty: in his efforts to centralize power, the emir moved against some of these same figures and imprisoned Aminullah. His relationship with Dost Mohammad is therefore one of rivalry and repression following shared resistance, rather than permanent alliance. The selected sources do not establish precise birth and death dates.

Related places: Logar, Kabul, Kohistan

7. Dost Mohammad Khan

Dost Mohammad · Card region: Kabul

December 1792–9 June 1863

1826–1863; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Some entries date his return to 1842; the specialist entry on the war and NAM date his return to Kabul to 1843.

Dost Mohammad, the son of Payinda Khan of the Barakzai family, established control over Kabul in the 1820s; his brothers held Kandahar, while Herat had a separate government. Recovering Peshawar from the Sikhs became central to his policy. His negotiations with Burnes and his acceptance of Russian contact should be understood as a search for support, rather than evidence of unchanging allegiance to an empire.

The invasion of 1839 drove him from Kabul; he subsequently surrendered to the British and went to India. After the occupation ended, he returned to Kabul and expanded his territory through warfare, family connections, and the containment of rivals until 1863. The capture of Herat shortly before his death marked the culmination of this process. His authority emerged through negotiation and violence among regional centres of power; a unified nation-state should not be assumed to have existed from the outset of his rule.

Related places: Kandahar, Kabul, Peshawar, Bukhara, Herat

8. Kabul Bazaar

Kabul Bazaar · Card region: Kabul

Kabul's bazaar was a meeting point for goods, credit, and news. Trade links with Peshawar and India through the Khyber, and with the mountains and Central Asia, sustained this landlocked capital. Merchants and caravan travellers carried information as well as merchandise.

The bazaar is more than a decorative backdrop in this account: its destruction by British forces in 1842 shows how political punishment could target urban livelihoods. That historical event appears on the timeline separately from the enduring institution of the bazaar itself.

9. Afghan Handicrafts

Afghan Handicrafts · Card region: Kabul

The handicrafts card refers to workshops, skills, and urban production, rather than a single factory or guild. Weaving, metalworking, and the manufacture of everyday goods formed part of an economy sustained by markets and caravan routes.

A historical reading must distinguish local production from imported goods: the presence of Indian or European textiles and manufactures in Kabul does not mean they were all made in Afghanistan. This entry explains the card's economic background without claiming specific production figures for the 1830s.

12. City of Ghazni

City of Ghazni · Card region: Kabul

1839–1839; Selected chronological connection, not the subject's date of origin

Ghazni is a historic city on the route between Kabul and Kandahar, and its significance extends beyond its fortress. The city depended on movement, provisioning, and control of a road that invading armies also had to take into account.

In the summer of 1839, the Army of the Indus's advance towards Kabul depended on capturing Ghazni. This card represents the city; card 17 concerns its fortifications. Distinguishing them is essential to separating the story of urban life from the story of battle.

13. Ghilzai Nomads

Ghilzai Nomads · Card region: Kabul

The Ghilzais comprised a large grouping of Pashtun lineages and communities. Some practised seasonal migration between Afghanistan's highlands and the plains near Sindh. Transporting goods and herding could be intertwined in these movements.

The card does not make all Ghilzais nomads. During the war of 1839–1842, some Ghilzai groups played important roles in resistance and attacks on lines of communication; tribal affiliation should not, however, be taken to imply permanent political loyalty.

14. Money Lenders

Money Lenders · Card region: Kabul

Moneylenders and money changers made it possible to transfer funds, extend credit, and settle obligations in an economy where transporting cash over long distances was difficult. Financial power here formed part of a network of social relationships.

16. Bala Hissar

Bala Hissar · Card region: Kabul

The Bala Hissar was Kabul's fortress and royal residence, a place where control of the city and the display of authority came together. Its structure changed across periods; it was not a building constructed in a single phase.

During the First Afghan War, the decision to station British troops outside this fortress contributed to their vulnerability. The Bala Hissar must be distinguished from the military cantonment outside the city. The image of the royal hall on the Dominance Check cards likewise evokes this relationship between architecture and power.

17. Citadel of Ghazni

Citadel of Ghazni · Card region: Kabul

1839–1839; Selected chronological connection, not the subject's date of origin

Ghazni's citadel was the defensive position blocking the invasion route to Kabul. On 23 July 1839, the force commanded by John Keane captured it by blowing open a gate and storming the fortress.

Information gathered by Mohan Lal helped identify a way into the defences. This battle is therefore more than a story of firepower: local intelligence, command decisions, and urban fortifications converged in its outcome.

18. Harry Flashman — fictional character

Harry Flashman · Card region: Punjab

Literary character; first appeared in 1857, reworked in the novel Flashman in 1969

His entire fictional biography and fictional relationships have been excluded from the historical relationship network.

Harry Flashman was not a real figure in the history of Afghanistan or Punjab. He was a character in Thomas Hughes's school novel Tom Brown's School Days, whom George MacDonald Fraser subsequently made the narrator of a series of historical novels. Fraser's first novel, Flashman, appeared in 1969. The publisher likewise identifies the series as historical fiction. Its memoir format and the character's presence among real events should not be mistaken for documentary authenticity. [1,2]

In a historical reading of Pax Pamir, this card belongs in a layer separate from authentic documents and biographies. His relationships with real people within the novels are not historical relationships that can be plotted in the network; accordingly, no verified edges connect Flashman to real individuals. This distinction also helps clarify narrative method: literature can reflect imperial imagination, hero-making, and criticism of empire, but it is not independent evidence of what a real individual did.

Rather than inventing dates of birth or military service for him, the timeline marks the card's literary position as fictional. The dates 1857 and 1969 refer to the character's publication and development, rather than his presence in nineteenth-century political history.

24. Ranjit Singh

Ranjit Singh · Card region: Punjab

1780–1839

1799–1839; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

By capturing Lahore in 1799 and assuming the title of maharaja in 1801, Ranjit Singh built a centralized power from among the Sikh political units. His realm was not confined to a single community or military practice; the Lahore court drew on administrators and soldiers of varied backgrounds. The treaty with Britain in 1809 constrained the direction of his expansion but also created an opportunity to concentrate on the north and west.

Sikh advances in Kashmir and Peshawar directly conflicted with the interests of Afghan rulers. Dost Mohammad wanted to recover Peshawar, and this demand became important in his negotiations with Britain. Allard and Avitabile were foreign officers serving the Lahore government, while Hari Singh Nalwa was an important commander on the western frontier. Punjab should therefore not be seen merely as a field of British–Russian rivalry: the Sikh state was an independent actor that sometimes cooperated with others and sometimes constrained them. Ranjit Singh's death in 1839 made this balance vulnerable.

74. Hajj Mirza Aghasi

Hajj Mirza Aghasi · Card region: Iran

Before becoming chief minister, Aghasi was Mohammad Mirza's tutor and spiritual guide. This personal trust became political power after his pupil became king: following Qa'em Maqam's removal in 1835, Aghasi remained at the head of government until Mohammad Shah's reign ended in 1848. 75. Abbas Mirza

Abbas Mirza · Card region: Iran

1789–1833

1799–1833; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Abbas Mirza, Fath-Ali Shah's son, became crown prince and governor of Azerbaijan in 1799. Tabriz's position near the Caucasus placed him on the front line of the Russo-Persian wars. Reorganizing the army, employing foreign instructors, and seeking new knowledge and equipment were among his responses to this pressure; the wars' outcome nevertheless showed the limits of military change without financial resources and stable political organization.

1797–1834; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Fath-Ali Shah's reign was a period both of Qajar dynastic consolidation and of the loss of large parts of the Caucasian territories to Russia. This transition shows how dynastic legitimacy, bureaucratic support, and the positions of foreign powers simultaneously shaped a royal succession.

After the death of his father, Abbas Mirza, Mohammad Mirza became the heir and succeeded his grandfather Fath-Ali Shah in 1834. 86. Jan Prosper Witkiewicz

Jan Prosper Witkiewicz · Card region: Transcaspia / Central Asia

1808–1839

1835–1839; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Accounts of a covert mission to undermine Russia and the precise motive for his death remain uncertain.

Witkiewicz, known in Russian as Yan Viktorovich Vitkevich, came from a Polish–Lithuanian family. After being arrested in connection with a secret anti-government society, he was exiled to service on the Orenburg frontier. Learning regional languages and working as an interpreter transformed him from an exile into an intelligence and diplomatic agent of the Russian Empire. The Orenburg History Museum's account emphasizes this career trajectory. [1]

His Kabul mission in 1837 brought him into competition with Alexander Burnes and to the negotiating table with Dost Mohammad. This competition should not be equated with an established, ready-to-execute Russian plan to invade India. Alexander Morrison's research shows that the gap between official perceptions, rumour, and the empires' practical capabilities was an important part of the crisis. [2]

He died after returning to St Petersburg in 1839; the usual account describes his death as suicide. Later interpretations about double agency or his hidden motives are not treated as established fact in this dashboard. [1,2]

91. Allah Quli Bahadur, Khan of Khiva

Allah Quli Bahadur · Card region: Transcaspia / Central Asia

Reigned 1825–1842

1825–1842; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

The release of Russian captives in 1840 did not amount to the general abolition of slavery.

Allah Quli Khan of the Qungrat dynasty ruled Khwarazm, a state more often called the Khanate of Khiva in European writing. Khiva was an important political and religious centre, but Khwarazm's commerce depended on a wider network, including Urgench. Allah Quli's rule is known for more than warfare: the madrasa bearing his name in Khiva, dated to 1835, provides material evidence of court patronage of architecture and religious institutions. His coins likewise record claims to sovereignty and the political language of the period. [1,2]

His most important confrontation with Russia occurred in the winter of 1839–1840, when Perovsky set out from Orenburg for Khiva, but extreme cold and the collapse of supplies prevented the Russian army from reaching it. Russian captives, attacks on caravans, and influence in the Kazakh steppe formed part of the conflict's background. [3]

Subsequently, releasing Russian captives and restricting their capture became means of reducing the likelihood of another invasion. This should not be equated with the complete abolition of slavery in Khwarazm; slavery and human trafficking continued beyond the question of Russian subjects. The khan's card thus has clear historical connections to Perovsky, the Khivan slave market, and supplies from Orenburg. [4]

93. Madali Khan (Mohammad Ali Khan of Kokand)

Madali Khan · Card region: Transcaspia / Central Asia

Reigned 1822–1842; killed in 1842

Mohammad Ali, known as Madali Khan, ruled Kokand after Umar Khan. Rooted in the Fergana Valley, the khanate became a far-reaching power during his reign, extending across routes through the steppe, Tashkent, the Pamirs, and the frontiers of Chinese Turkestan. Its expansion was not solely military: fort-building, taxation, and trade with territories under Chinese rule strengthened the state. His card thus represents a local actor with independent aims who cannot simply be regarded as a Russian or British pawn. [1]

Territorial growth and increasing fiscal pressure coincided with competition among court factions and local discontent. Moralizing stories about the khan's personal life alone do not adequately explain the power structures behind his eventual defeat. The government installed by Bukhara did not last long, and local opponents rallied around Sher Ali Khan. The swift emergence of resistance after Madali's fall shows that Kokand's fate was shaped not only by the will of emirs, but also by alliances and responses within regional society. The card's main connection in the network is war and rivalry with Nasrullah Khan. [2]

95. Supplies from Orenburg

Supplies from Orenburg · Card region: Transcaspia / Central Asia

1839–1840; Selected chronological connection, not the subject's date of origin

Orenburg was more than a point on a map of Russia: it was a base for supplying food, fodder, pack animals, drivers, and information for movement across the steppe. Alexander Morrison's research shows that Russian and British armies in Central Asia during the first half of the nineteenth century still depended on animal transport. Camels and the skills needed to manage them came largely from local pastoral communities—the very people who sometimes faced imperial pressure and expansion. Military success therefore depended on negotiation, payment, and local cooperation, not simply on guns and cannon. [1]

Perovsky's expedition to Khiva provides a clear example of the card's historical meaning. Stockpiling dried bread, meat, winter clothing, and animal feed in 1839 was a political and financial undertaking. On the march, each camel carrying supplies needed to be fed, so part of the carrying capacity sustained the transport system itself. Severe cold and animal deaths immobilised the Russian force before it reached Khiva. The card should be read alongside Perovsky, the khan of Khiva, and the question of trade routes: logistics set the practical limits of imperial power. [1,2]

97. Ark of Bukhara

Ark of Bukhara · Card region: Transcaspia / Central Asia

The connection between the Ark and the missions is historical and institutional; the card's title does not automatically establish the precise location of imprisonment or execution.

The Ark of Bukhara was the principal residence of the city's rulers and retained that role until 1920. What survives is not the product of a single construction date: the complex was repeatedly destroyed, rebuilt, and extended. Bukhara Museum's introduction lists audience and throne halls, a mosque, government officials' courtyards, a treasury, an arsenal, storerooms, and courtiers' residences among its components. Within Pax Pamir's historical setting, the Ark was a material concentration of the emir's power: a place where reception ceremonies, state administration, and the display of distance between ruler and foreign envoy came together. This interpretation clarifies the card's thematic connection with Nasrullah Khan and the missions of Stoddart and Conolly. However, the image of the Ark does not justify locating every prison, act of torture, or execution site in Bukhara inside the complex. Bukhara's World Heritage dossier also places the Ark within the city's history and identifies it as a residence of its rulers. This card therefore represents an enduring historical institution and place, rather than an event that can be assigned a single year on a timeline. [3]

100. Count Perovsky (Vasily Perovsky)

Count Perovsky · Card region: Transcaspia / Central Asia

1795–1857; some catalogues give 1794 as his birth year

1833–1857; Selected period of activity, not the whole lifespan

Sources give 1794/1795 for his birth; the Library of Congress description and its heading disagree.

Perovsky was a Russian commander and frontier administrator at Orenburg. The city occupied a gateway position between Russian territory, the Kazakh steppe, and the Central Asian khanates. The episode most closely associated with his card is the winter expedition against Khiva in 1839–1840. Disputes over caravan security, Russian captives, and authority over routes were evident causes of the expedition; imperial influence and prestige also shaped the decision. [1,2]

Morrison's research places this operation alongside British intervention in Afghanistan and uses the failures of both empires to challenge the idea of their inevitable advance. 1840 · Logistical collapse and the Russian retreat

Cold and disease halted the Russian army before it reached Khiva. The order to return was issued in February; this failure was not the same as a battlefield victory by the khan of Khiva's army.

Related cards: 100, 91, 95

  1. Morrison, Pray for the Camels

1840-11 · Dost Mohammad surrenders and is taken to India

After attempting to regain power, Dost Mohammad surrendered to the British; his absence created opportunities for Akbar Khan and other resistance leaders.

Related cards: 7, 3

  1. National Army Museum: First Afghan War

1841-11-02 · The Kabul uprising and the killing of Burnes

Burnes was killed during the Kabul uprising, and the occupation administration faced a fundamental crisis. Various forces and leaders played a part in it.

Related cards: 21, 1, 6, 5, 40, 112

  1. Encyclopaedia Iranica; Malcolm Yapp: Alexander Burnes

1841-12-12 · The end of Zorawar Singh's Tibetan campaign

Dogra expansion from Ladakh and Baltistan into western Tibet was halted by winter defeat and Zorawar's death. This war should be distinguished from the contemporaneous war in Afghanistan.

Related cards: 33, 27

  2. Inderjeet Singh, Revisiting Zorawar Singh's Campaign in Tibet, The Tibet Journal 43/1, 2018

1841-12-23 · The killing of Macnaghten

Macnaghten was killed at a meeting with Akbar Khan, marking a major breakdown in negotiations between the garrison and Afghan forces.

Related cards: 40, 3, 19

  1. National Army Museum: William Hay Macnaghten, collection record

1842 · Nasrullah captures Kokand and Madali is killed

Bukhara took Kokand, and Madali and members of his family were killed. The government installed by Bukhara soon faced local resistance, and Shir Ali Khan rose to power.

Related cards: 90, 93

  1. Şen, Kokand under Shir Ali Khan

1842-01-06 to 1842-01-13 · The retreat from Kabul and the destruction of the column

Soldiers and civilian followers suffered cold, attacks, and logistical collapse on the route to Jalalabad. There were numerous hostages and survivors; the claim that 'only one person survived' is incorrect when applied to the entire population.

Related cards: 3, 19, 32, 36, 53, 29

  1. Dictionary of Irish Biography, Royal Irish Academy: Eldred Pottinger
  2. National Army Museum: First Afghan War

1842-04-05 · The killing of Shah Shuja

Shah Shuja's death after the garrison's departure ended his restoration; the struggle over Kabul continued.

Related cards: 5, 3, 16

1842-06-17 · The execution of Stoddart and Conolly in Bukhara

Prison letters and Wolff's later account are two different kinds of evidence for this episode. The date of 17 June follows the Library of Congress description.

1842-10-12 · The departure of the punitive forces from Kabul

After re-entering Kabul, freeing prisoners, and destroying its bazaar, Pollock's and Nott's forces withdrew from Afghanistan.

1843 · Dost Mohammad returns to power in Kabul

The emir's return began a new phase of consolidating power and confronting domestic rivals.

1843 to 1845 · Wolff's journey to establish the two officers' fate

The journey took place after the two officers' deaths; the account was published in 1845.

1849-03-29 · Punjab is annexed by the East India Company

After the Second Sikh War, Punjab was annexed on 29 March 1849. This happened after Ranjit Singh's death and should not be attributed to him. The Koh-i-Noor was also removed from the treasury under imposed terms of surrender.

Related cards: 24, 30, 38, 109, 106

  1. National Army Museum: Second Sikh War
  2. Royal Collection Trust: Duleep Singh and the Koh-i-Noor

1863 · The conquest of Herat and the death of Dost Mohammad

Dost Mohammad incorporated Herat into his territory and died in June of the same year. This continued the process of consolidating power and was distinct from the Qajar siege of 1837–1838.

Related cards: 7, 56

  1. Encyclopaedia Iranica; Amin Tarzi: Dost Mohammad Khan

1870 · A geographical medal and the end of Hayward's journey

The Royal Geographical Society awarded Hayward its Founder's Medal; Rawlinson received it on his behalf. Hayward was killed later on his journey in the Yasin and Darkot area; this belongs to a later period than the First Afghan War.

Related cards: 22, 20

  1. JRGS 40 (1870), medal ceremony
  2. Letters from Mr. Hayward, 1870–1871

1885 · The Panjdeh crisis: a later chapter in frontier rivalry

A clash between Russian and Afghan forces over the Panjdeh oasis led to a diplomatic crisis. Its setting differed from that of the 1830s; late nineteenth-century boundary-making forms part of this story.
